The No-Nonsense Hypertrophy Guide

All the science, none of the jargon (and hopefully a laugh or two)
By Maria Paradise · mpfcoaching.co.uk

The 60-second version

  1. Growth comes from tension. Muscles grow when they're challenged under load, not when they're "torn".
  2. Pick a weight you can lift for 6–30 reps, then push each working set to within 0–3 reps of failure.
  3. Aim for roughly 10–20 hard sets per muscle per week, spread across your sessions.
  4. Control the weight. Full range, a proper stretch, no swinging, no bouncing.
  5. Do a bit more over time (weight, reps or sets), and write it down so you actually know.
  6. Form before load. Always.

01

Quick terms before we start

Total beginner? Start here. None of this is complicated, but gym language throws people off before they've even started, so let's clear it up in one go.

  • Rep (repetition): one full movement, e.g. one bicep curl, up and down.
  • Set: a group of reps done back to back. "3 sets of 10" means 10 reps, rest, 10 more, rest, 10 more.
  • 1RM (one-rep max): the heaviest weight you could lift once with good form. Mostly a reference point, not something you train at often.
  • Compound exercise: uses more than one joint and muscle group, e.g. a squat.
  • Isolation exercise: targets one muscle through one joint, e.g. a bicep curl.
  • Volume: the total work you do, usually sets × reps × weight, per exercise or across the week.
  • Failure: the point where you can't do another rep with good form. Your face will make a noise it doesn't normally make.

Everything else (RIR, RPE, tempo and so on) gets explained in plain English in its own section before it's ever used to tell you what to do.

02

This is a "get bigger" guide, not a "lift heavier" guide

Same gym, completely different job

Hypertrophy (bigger muscles) and strength (a bigger 1-rep max) are cousins, not twins. A powerlifter and a bodybuilder can share a rack and still be training almost completely different systems, a bit like a marathon runner and a sprinter who both "run" but would be useless swapping races. Mixing the two up is one of the most common reasons people train hard for months and barely change shape.

What actually drives each one

Hypertrophy is driven mainly by mechanical tension: the pulling force a muscle fibre feels under load, which the cell turns into a "build more protein" signal. Enough of that signal, applied consistently over weeks, makes a muscle physically bigger.1

Strength is mostly a skill and nervous-system problem. Early strength gains come overwhelmingly from your brain and spinal cord getting better at recruiting more fibres, firing them faster and coordinating them on that specific lift.22,23 That's why a beginner's squat can shoot up in the first few months with barely any visible muscle growth, and why strength is quite specific to the exact exercise trained, while size transfers to the muscle whichever exercise built it.24

03

The three pillars of hypertrophy

You don't need to "tear" your muscles to grow them. Your muscles don't know what a bicep curl is. All they know is "something heavy just tried to move me, I'd better get bigger so that's easier next time." Your muscles are not smart, but they are extremely committed to not being embarrassed twice. Growth is driven mostly by mechanical tension and metabolic stress (the "pump"), with muscle damage playing a much smaller role than gym folklore suggests.1 On top of overall volume and progressive overload, three variables come up again and again.

Pillar 1 · Mind-muscle connection

Deliberately focusing on the muscle you're working genuinely increases its activity, especially with light-to-moderate weights (up to about 60% of your max).2,3 Load the bar close to your max and it stops mattering. There's no spare brainpower for "feeling the squeeze" when you're busy not getting crushed. To be honest about the evidence: it mostly shows more electrical activity in the muscle, not proof of more growth.4

  • Use it on lighter isolation moves: bicep curls, not your 1RM deadlift
  • Slow the lowering slightly and actually squeeze at the top
  • Don't try to "feel" a heavy compound lift. Just lift it (and maybe pray a little)

Pillar 2 · Full range of motion

Full range beats partial reps for growth, on average.5 The newer research is fussier: reps done in the deep stretch (the bottom of a squat, not the top) can match or even beat full range for some muscles.6,7 The style that consistently comes last is the shortened partial, those tiny half-reps at the top of a curl.

  • Default to full range with a proper stretch at the bottom
  • "Lengthened partials" (half reps staying low in the stretch) make a handy finisher, but only at the end of a set, once you can't do another full rep. They follow full range of motion, they never replace it
  • Quarter reps at the top are ego reps. Nobody's impressed, they're busy filming their own set.

Pillar 3 · Time under tension

Reps lasting anywhere from about half a second to 8 seconds build muscle equally well, as long as sets go close to failure and total work is the same.8 So you can stop the dramatic "one Mississippi" count out loud. What doesn't work is turning every rep into a 10-second ordeal, mostly because you have to use a much lighter weight to survive it.8

Bottom line: a controlled, non-bouncy rep is all you need. Total effort and volume matter far more than the clock.
04

Don't let your muscles clock off mid-set

Tension is the pulling force your muscle feels while holding or moving a weight, and it's what tells your body to grow. Any moment in a rep where that tension drops to near zero is a moment that isn't working for you. Locking out fully can shift the load onto bone and connective tissue, and a dead stop at the bottom lets the weight hang on your joints instead of your muscle.

How people accidentally break tension

  • Locking out fully on isolation moves like curls, pushdowns and leg extensions
  • Letting the plates touch down on plate-loaded machines. The plates are having a lovely nap. Your muscle is not invited.
  • Using momentum: swinging, hip-thrusting or bouncing the weight up
  • Quitting the moment it gets uncomfortable. That discomfort is the stimulus, not a reason to stop

Simple fixes

  • Stop just short of full lockout on isolation moves
  • No dead stops, and stop just before the plates touch (unless a pause is planned)
  • Move the weight with the muscle, not with body English
  • Squeeze at peak contraction instead of passing straight through it
  • Push through the burn towards genuine failure or near-failure
COACH'S NOTEOn heavy compound lifts (squat, deadlift, bench), a controlled lockout is still the safer, correct technique. Save the "no lockout" cue for isolation and machine work, not your max squat.
05

Tempo: how to actually use it

Tempo is written as four numbers: lowering – pause at the bottom – lifting – pause at the top. So 3-1-1-0 means 3 seconds down, 1 second pause in the stretch, 1 second up, no pause at the top. It looks like maths homework, but nobody's checking your working.

  • Lowering: 2–4 seconds, under control. Free tension for nothing
  • Pause at the bottom: 0–2 seconds. Kills the bounce, keeps you honest
  • Lifting: push like you mean it, even if the weight moves slowly

Tempo has a wide "it doesn't really matter" window, so treat it as a tool for technique and focus, not something to lose sleep over.8

06

Rep ranges: 6–30

A weight-picker, not a finish line

Rep ranges help you pick a weight. They don't tell you when to rack it. "6–12 reps" is a guide to how heavy the bar should be, not a red light that says stop even though you've got two good reps left. What decides when the set's done is how close you are to failure.

Research shows similar growth from light and heavy loads when sets are pushed hard, anywhere from roughly 30% to 85%+ of your max (broadly 6–30+ reps).9,10 How close you get to failure predicts growth better than the rep number does.

RangeGood forWhy
6–12Compound liftsEfficient, more mechanical tension
12–20Accessory workGood pump, easier on joints
20–30Isolation / machinesGoing heavy would be awkward or risky
COACH'S NOTEIf you rack the bar the second you hit 12 even though it felt easy, you've turned a useful guideline into an arbitrary rule. Your muscles don't know you hit a nice round number. They just know whether they were challenged.
07

RIR & RPE: how to measure effort

Reps in reserve (RIR) is your best guess at how many more reps you could have done before true failure. "I could've done two more" = 2 RIR. Nothing left at all = 0 RIR.20

RPE (rate of perceived exertion) is RIR flipped onto a 1–10 scale, where 10 means failure.21 It's handy because your "max" isn't fixed. It changes with sleep, stress and whatever life throws at you, and RPE lets you adjust on the day.

08

Warm-ups, and how I coach working sets

One general warm-up + warm-up sets at RPE ~6

A general warm-up, then a couple of warm-up ("feeder") sets at about RPE 6 (you could do about 4 more reps) before your working weight. That preps your joints and nervous system without burning energy you need for the real work.

Then: two proper working sets, as close to failure as possible

That's my go-to: 2 working sets at 0–3 RIR, as close to failure as you safely can. Hard enough that the last rep isn't pretty. Think "concerned facial expression", not "gentle Sunday stroll". It's one good method among many. Straight sets, RIR-based sets, drop sets and more sets at lighter effort all apply the same science, and the best one depends on your recovery, schedule, injury history and how your week's going.

Straps and belts? Only when you actually need them

Use lifting straps, wrist wraps or a belt only when it's genuinely necessary, like your heaviest pulling sets when your grip is failing before your back. Otherwise go without, so your grip and core get stronger too. Feeling weak, tired, on your period or not 100% today? A bit of help is fine, as long as it's a conscious decision, not a habit. Common sense, basically.

Why two sets is enough (when it is)

Two sets per exercise only works because it's never the only work a muscle gets that week. Across a full programme covering every major movement (squat, hinge, push, pull, carry) and hitting each muscle from more than one angle, those sets add up to the 10–20 hard sets per muscle per week that research links to solid growth.25 Two sets is a per-exercise dose, not a per-week one.

When two sets is not enough

  • A muscle only appears in one exercise, once a week. That's under-dosed, not efficient
  • You're a complete beginner still learning technique. Extra lighter practice sets matter more than chasing failure
  • A specific muscle is lagging and needs direct extra volume
  • Recovery is poor (illness, bad sleep, high stress) and the body can't absorb it that week

How long should you rest?

There's no perfect number of seconds. Rest until you physically feel ready to take the next set close to failure again: your breathing has settled, the muscle doesn't feel burnt out, and you could genuinely push it as hard as the last one. Bigger lifts usually need longer than small ones. Rushing back in tired just means fewer good reps, which means less growth. Resting is not the same as scrolling for ten minutes, though.

09

Why I make every client use a logbook

You can't progress what you don't measure. Without a written record, "did I lift more than last week?" is a guess, and guesses drift. Most people who think they've plateaued haven't. They've lost track and repeated the same numbers for weeks.

It also fixes the RIR problem. If your top set has read "8 reps, felt like a 7" for a month, that's a fact I can act on. Without the log, it's just a vibe. Vibes don't build muscle. Mechanical tension does.

What to log

  • Exercise, weight and reps for every working set
  • A quick effort rating (RPE or RIR) per set
  • Anything unusual: bad sleep, high stress, a niggle, feeling great
10

Progressive overload: the "line must go up" rule

Progressive overload means gradually asking a bit more of your muscles over time. Some progression is necessary for continued growth, though it isn't perfectly linear, and just "doing more" won't work forever once recovery or nutrition become the bottleneck.14 Grunting loudly does not count as a training variable.

  • Weight: add load, same rep range
  • Reps: add reps at the same weight before bumping the load
  • Sets: add volume to a lagging muscle
  • Control: deeper stretch or less momentum, same weight
  • Density: same work, slightly less rest

Adding load and adding reps produced basically the same gains over 8 weeks in a direct comparison, so adding reps when the next weight jump feels too big is a legitimate strategy, not a cop-out.15

11

Training to failure: the full story

  • True failure: you genuinely can't do another rep with decent form. The bar wins
  • Technical failure: your form goes before true failure. The safer stopping point on heavy compounds

Pushing closer to failure is thought to recruit more of the fibres that respond best to growth. The stubborn ones that only dance after the third song. A 2023 meta-analysis found a real but small edge for sets taken closer to failure,12 but an 8-week trial comparing true failure with stopping 1–2 reps short found essentially identical growth, with much more fatigue in the failure group.13

How to apply it

  • Most sets: stop at 0–3 RIR
  • Save true failure for isolation moves, machines and the last set of an exercise
  • Go easy on taking heavy squats, deadlifts and bench to true failure. The risk-to-reward isn't great
COACH'S NOTEChange is genuinely possible on your own. What a coach adds isn't secret knowledge. It's knowing exactly when to adapt your training as you progress, plateau, or life gets in the way.
12

Drop sets & post-exhaust: when to bother

Drop sets

Hit failure, drop the weight by about 20–25%, and keep going with barely a breath. Two meta-analyses found trivial differences in growth versus normal sets, but drop sets cut training time by roughly 30–70%.11,16 They won't make you grow faster, but they'll get you out of the gym faster.

  • Use when short on time, or for extra volume without extra sets
  • Best on the last set of an isolation or machine move
  • One or two per session is plenty. The fatigue bill adds up fast

Post-exhaust (and pre-exhaust)

Post-exhaust pairs a compound lift with an isolation finisher for the same muscle (leg press, then leg extensions). That's really just the sensible rule of doing big lifts first.17 Pre-exhaust (isolation first, e.g. leg extensions before squats) builds muscle about as well as normal exercise order in the research, not better and not worse.18,19 So why do I still use it? Because the muscle is already tired going into the big lift, you can get a hard set with less weight on the bar, which is kinder on your joints and lower back, and it adds extra work for a muscle that needs it. What it doesn't do is make the target muscle "work harder" in the compound. Studies measuring muscle activity found it doesn't increase, and helper muscles (like your triceps on a chest press) can actually end up doing more.18 It's a useful tool, not a hack. Expect to use a bit less weight on the compound afterwards. That's normal.

Don't expect a secret hack. Total volume and effort still do the heavy lifting (pun intended).
13

Safety first (yes, even in a guide with jokes)

Training hard and training safely aren't in competition. Chasing failure on a heavy squat with sloppy form is how "pushing close to failure" turns into "pushing the ambulance call button".

  • Form before load, always. A lighter weight done properly beats a heavier one done badly
  • Know fatigue from pain. A burning, tired muscle is normal. Sharp, sudden or joint pain is not, so stop the set
  • Use safety equipment on heavy lifts: spotter arms, a spotter or a Smith machine. Pride has crushed more people than barbells.
  • Warm up properly and progress load gradually
  • Get technique coaching early. Bad habits get heavier the longer they stick

Get medical clearance first if you…

have a heart, joint or musculoskeletal condition, are recovering from injury or surgery, are pregnant, or are new to exercise after a long break. Nothing in this guide overrides advice from a medical professional who knows your history.

Stop immediately and seek help if you get

  • Sudden, sharp or shooting pain during a lift
  • Chest pain, dizziness, or breathlessness beyond normal hard-set breathing
  • A joint that gives way, clicks painfully or swells after training
  • An injury that doesn't improve after a few days' rest
14

Why this guide alone isn't a programme

Everything above is solid science, but science gives you principles, not a personalised plan. Your body didn't read the textbook. Frankly, it can't read at all. The details need building around you:

  • Your proportions: limb length and joint set-up change which exercises suit you
  • Your lifestyle: how many days you can train and how well you recover
  • Your injuries: the textbook version of an exercise isn't always right for your joints
  • Your nutrition: without the right fuel, the training has nothing to build with

Two people can follow the same evidence-based principles and get very different results, purely because one plan was built around them and the other wasn't.
