The No-Nonsense Strength Guide

How to actually lift heavier, without wrecking yourself in the process
By Maria Paradise · mpfcoaching.co.uk

The 60-second version

  1. Strength is a skill. Your nervous system learns to use more of your muscle, faster, on that exact lift.
  2. Lift heavy: 1–5 reps (think 3×3, 3×5, 5×5) at roughly 80–90%+ of your max.
  3. Go hard: 0–3 reps in reserve. You can take a set all the way to failure only when it's safe (safety bars, a spotter or a machine). Never full failure on a deadlift.
  4. It's not about feeling the muscle or chasing tension. It's about lifting something heavy for a few reps, with perfect technique. Ramp up your warm-up, brace hard, lift with intent.
  5. Rest properly: usually 3–5 minutes, until you're fully ready for another crisp set.
  6. Hit all your reps, then add a little weight. Repeat for months. That's it. That's the secret.

01

Strength sets and hypertrophy sets are different jobs

Most of your programme is built for hypertrophy: making muscles bigger. Some exercises are strength work: making you able to lift more. Same gym, same barbell, completely different rules. Treat your heavy triples like a pump set and you'll end up with neither.

COACH'S NOTEThey help each other. Bigger muscles have more potential to be strong, and getting stronger lets you use heavier weights for your hypertrophy work. That's why a lot of programmes have a bit of both.
02

What strength actually is

When you first start lifting heavy, your strength shoots up long before your muscles visibly grow. That's because strength is largely a nervous system skill.1 Your brain and spinal cord get better at:

  • Recruitment: switching on more of the muscle fibres you already have
  • Rate coding: firing those fibres faster, so they produce more force
  • Coordination: getting all the muscles involved in a lift working together, and the ones working against you to back off

Because it's a skill, strength is specific. Get strong at a low-bar squat and you get strong at a low-bar squat. Your leg press will improve a bit, but not by the same amount. Which is why "I can leg press the whole stack" means absolutely nothing under a barbell.

Over months and years, muscle size becomes a bigger part of the picture. A bigger muscle has more to work with. So you need both: practise the lift, and keep building the muscle.

03

Weight and reps: go heavy

You can build muscle with light or heavy weights, as long as you train hard. Strength is different: heavier loads build more strength, because you get better at what you practise.2,3 That means sets of roughly 1–5 reps with a weight that's genuinely heavy for you.

Here's roughly how reps line up with percentage of your one-rep max (1RM). Everyone's a bit different, so treat it as a guide, not gospel.

04

How hard: heavy, and failure only when it's safe

Strength sets are hard sets with heavy weight: aim for 0–3 reps in reserve (RPE 7–10).4 Unlike hypertrophy work, you're not chasing tension or the pump. You're trying to move a heavy weight for a few reps with the same technique on every single rep.

Can you go to failure on a strength set? Yes, but only when it's safe to do so. Research shows strength goes up about the same whether sets are taken right to failure or stopped a little short,5 so failure is allowed, not required. What matters is that a failed rep can't hurt you:

  • Safe to fail: back squat or front squat in a rack with the safety bars set, bench press with a spotter or safety arms, machines like the leg press with the stops set
  • Be really careful: overhead press (only with safeties), weighted pull-ups (step down under control, never drop with a heavy belt)
  • Don't take to full failure: deadlifts (any style). A failed deadlift usually means a rounded back under a heavy bar. Stop the set when the bar slows right down

Nobody ever got strong by folding in half under a deadlift.

05

Ramp-up warm-ups

Heavy sets need a proper build-up. You're warming up your joints and muscles, but you're also rehearsing the lift so your first working set feels familiar rather than terrifying. The trick is lots of small steps with fewer reps as the weight goes up, so you arrive ready, not tired.

    06

    Bracing and breathing

    On heavy lifts, a solid brace is non-negotiable. Taking a big breath and tightening your whole middle builds pressure inside your abdomen, which stiffens your torso so the force from your legs and arms goes into the bar instead of leaking out through a wobbly middle.6

    • Breathe in deep into your belly and the sides of your waist, not up into your chest
    • Tighten all the way round, like you're about to be punched in the stomach
    • Ribs down over your hips, no arching
    • Hold it through the whole rep, breathe out at the top, then re-brace before every single rep
    SAFETYHolding your breath under heavy load spikes your blood pressure for a few seconds. If you have high blood pressure, a heart condition, or you're pregnant, check with your GP before doing heavy braced lifting.
    07

    Move with intent, and think about the movement

    Lower the weight under control, then try to lift it as fast as you possibly can. On a heavy set it won't actually move fast, and that's fine. Research shows it's the intent to move quickly that drives the training effect, not how fast the bar really goes.7

    On hypertrophy sets you focus on feeling the muscle. On strength sets, flip it: think about the movement and what you're doing to the outside world. Focusing externally like this tends to produce more force and better technique.8

    • Squat and leg press: "push the floor away"
    • Bench: "push yourself away from the bar" into the bench
    • Deadlift: "keep the bar close and push the floor away"
    • Overhead press: "head through the window" as the bar passes your face
    08

    Rest: longer than you think

    Heavy sets drain your muscles and your nervous system. Research on rest periods shows strength improves more with longer rests (more than 2 minutes) than with short ones.9 For most heavy compound lifts that means 3–5 minutes.

    There's no countdown timer in your logbook, because there's no magic number. Rest until you're fully recovered and you could hit every rep of the next set with the same weight and the same perfect form. Rest is not the same as scrolling for ten minutes, though. I can see your step count.

    09

    Technique and kit

    Make every rep identical

    Strength is a skill, so practise the same skill every time: same foot position, same grip, same depth, same bar path. Film a set from the side every now and then and send it to me. Form before load. Always.

    Straps, wraps and belts: only when you actually need them

    My rule is simple: use kit only when it's genuinely necessary. Every time a strap holds the bar for you, your grip doesn't get stronger. Every time a belt does the bracing, your core doesn't. Use them all the time and you end up needing them.

    The exception: days you're feeling weak, tired, on your period, run down, or just not at 100%. Then a bit of help so you can still train well is completely fine, as long as it's a conscious decision, not a habit. Basically: have some common sense.

    Lifting straps or figure-of-eights (for grip)

    • When: your heaviest pulling sets (deadlifts, heavy rows, heavy shrugs) when your grip is genuinely failing before the muscle you're training
    • Not: warm-ups, lighter sets, or "just because". And never on presses, you push a bench, you don't hang off it

    Wrist wraps (for wrist support)

    • When: your heaviest bench, overhead press or weighted dip sets if your wrists bend back or ache
    • Not: warm-ups, light sets or machines. They support the joint, they don't fix bad wrist position. Keep your wrists stacked over your elbows

    Lifting belts

    • When: only your very heaviest squat and deadlift sets, once you can already brace properly without one. The belt gives your brace something to push against
    • Not: as a replacement for learning to brace, on warm-ups, or on every set. If you can't brace without it, you're not ready for it

    Weighted pull-ups and chin-ups

    Pull-ups are a brilliant strength lift. Because you're lifting your own body, your "weight" is your bodyweight plus anything you add.

    • Earn the weight first: get to 3 sets of 5 strict bodyweight reps (dead hang, chin over the bar, no kipping) before adding any
    • Then add weight with a dip belt or a dumbbell between your feet, in small jumps of 1.25–2.5kg
    • Estimate your 1RM on the total (bodyweight + added weight), then subtract your bodyweight to see what to hang off the belt. The free 1RM calculator does this for you
    • Safety: step down onto a box under control. Never drop off the bar with a heavy belt on
    10

    Sets and frequency

    More hard sets per week does build more strength, up to a point.10 Training a lift more often helps too, mostly because it gives you more total practice.11 For most people that looks like:

    • 3–5 working sets of your main strength lift in a session
    • Practising each main lift 1–3 times a week
    • Your hypertrophy work after it, when you're fresh enough for the heavy stuff to go first
    Order matters: heavy strength work goes first in the session, when you're fresh. Do your heavy sets before anything that tires the muscle out.
    11

    Progression: how strength goes up

    The rule is simple. Hit all your sets and reps at the right effort, then add weight.

    • Got every rep of your 3×5 with good form? Next time add a little: usually 2.5kg on upper body lifts and 2.5–5kg on lower body lifts
    • Missed reps or form broke down? Stay at that weight until it's solid
    • Stuck at the same weight for 2–3 weeks? Tell me. We might drop the reps, add a set, or take a lighter week

    Over a training block, the plan usually changes as you get stronger: more sets of 5ish reps first, then fewer, heavier reps. Planning training in phases like this (periodisation) tends to beat doing the same thing forever.12 Beginners can add weight almost every week. Enjoy it, it doesn't last forever.

    12

    Pause reps and other strength tools

    Strength work doesn't need fancy intensity tricks. It needs heavy, clean, repeatable reps. But a few tools are genuinely useful, and they all have one thing in common: they make the reps better, not messier.

    Pause reps

    Stop completely at the hardest point of the lift for 1–3 seconds, stay braced, then drive up. On a squat that's the bottom, on a bench it's with the bar touching your chest, on a deadlift it's just below or just above the knee.

    • They take the bounce out, so you have to produce force from a dead stop in your weakest position
    • They force you to stay tight and braced, which cleans up technique fast
    • Use roughly 10–20% less weight than your normal sets for the same reps
    • Great for anyone who bounces out of the bottom, loses position halfway up, or rushes their reps

    Top set + back-off sets

    Work up to one heavier set (your top set) at about RPE 8, then drop the weight by about 10% for a few more sets of the same reps. You get the heavy practice and the extra quality volume without grinding.

    Cluster sets

    Split a heavy set into small chunks with 15–30 seconds rest between them, e.g. 5 reps done as 2 + 2 + 1. Every rep stays fast and crisp because fatigue never builds up. Handy when your technique falls apart towards the end of a set.

    Controlled eccentrics (negatives)

    The eccentric is the lowering part of the rep. Slow it right down, about 3–5 seconds on the way down, then lift with intent. You're stronger on the way down than on the way up, so negatives let you practise handling heavy weight in good positions and build control at the bottom.

    • Use about 10–20% less weight than your normal working sets
    • Great for pull-ups too: jump or step to the top, then lower yourself slowly. Brilliant if you're working towards your first strict pull-up
    • They make you properly sore, so keep them to a few sets and only use them when they're in your plan

    Periodisation and building up to a new max

    Your 1RM (one-rep max) is the heaviest weight you can lift once with good form. You don't need to test it often. You can estimate it from a heavy set using a standard formula: weight × (1 + reps ÷ 30).13 It's most accurate from sets of 10 reps or fewer, and the closer to failure the set, the better the estimate.

    Periodisation: planning the build-up

    Periodisation just means planning your training in phases instead of doing the same thing forever. For maximal strength, periodised programmes beat non-periodised ones.12 The main styles:

    • Linear: the weight goes up and the reps come down over weeks (e.g. 3×5 → 3×3 → singles). Simple and brilliant for most people
    • Block: a few weeks focused on one thing (building a base), then the next (heavier work), then peaking
    • Undulating: different rep ranges in the same week (e.g. a heavy 3×3 day and a lighter 3×6 day)

    Linear and undulating plans build strength about equally well,16 so the best one is the one that fits your week and that you'll actually stick to. Every few blocks there's also a lighter deload week, so fatigue drops and your strength can catch up and show.

    14

    When (and how) to test a 1RM

    When

    Most of the time, you don't need to. Your app shows an estimated 1RM from your logged sets, and if that's going up, you're getting stronger. A real max test only happens when:

    • It's in your plan. I'll put it at the end of a strength block, after a taper. Never freestyle one
    • You've been lifting consistently for at least 6 months and your technique on that lift is solid
    • You're well slept, well fed and healthy, ideally at maintenance or in a surplus, not deep in a fat loss phase
    • It's been at least 8–12 weeks since your last one

    Don't test if you're a beginner, injured, ill, run down, in a hard fat loss phase, or on a lift that isn't safe to max (single-leg work, isolation exercises).

    How

    A properly run 1RM test is a reliable way to measure strength.15 Properly run being the key words.

    • Use safety bars or a spotter. Every time. No exceptions
    • Warm up with your ramp-up sets, then singles at about 80%, 85% and 90% of your expected max
    • Take 2–3 attempts with 3–5 minutes rest between them, making each jump smaller
    • Stop as soon as one is a big grind or your form changes. A max with bad form doesn't count
    COACH'S NOTELots of people never need to test a true max. An estimated 1RM from a good heavy set tells you almost as much, with a fraction of the risk and fatigue.
